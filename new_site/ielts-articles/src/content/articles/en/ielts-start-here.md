> This is my personal experience, not official advice. Test formats, rules and fees change, so check the official British Council pages before you decide anything.

This is the first article in my IELTS series. Here I'll walk you through how I set things up before I started training: what to understand first, how to find your real level, how to pick your exam date, and how I practised. Then, article by article, we'll go through each part of the exam.

## Two things before anything else

**1. Understand the exam. Don't "study English."**
You're not here to memorize word lists or learn the language from scratch. You need to understand how the test is scored and how each type of question works. That is what actually moves your score.

**2. Pick your sources, and stick to them.**
My main source is the British Council. When you book your exam through them, you get a free preparation program (they call it Ready), and it has most of what you need. I'll support it with a few other sources, but the British Council stays the base. Check what your booking includes.

If you haven't booked yet, [IELTS Liz](https://ieltsliz.com/) is a great free source, and her [FAQ page](https://ieltsliz.com/ielts-help-faq/) is the best place to get the basics of the test.

One more thing: everything in this series is about the **computer-based** exam, not the other formats. The FAQ page explains the difference.

## Step 1: find your real starting level

The exam depends heavily on your English level and on how much you use English day to day. So before planning anything, find out where you stand.

For me: my listening and reading were good to average. In speaking I could hold a conversation. In writing I could write a bit, but I had forgotten a lot of grammar rules. Your vocabulary range matters too, so check how varied it really is.

How do you find out?

- Use the British Council's free [EnglishScore](https://www.britishcouncil.org/english/learn-online/englishscore) app.
- Take an online vocabulary test, like the [vocabulary size test](https://www.vocabularytester.com/vocabulary-test), and a couple of grammar tests.

Why more than one test? Because some are easy and some are hard, and they don't measure the same thing. Look at your **average** score. That's your baseline, and you'll use it to choose your exam date and leave yourself enough time to prepare.

## Step 2: choose your exam date

My personal opinion: leave yourself **at least a month** if you're around B1 to B2. You can take longer, of course, depending on your situation, especially if you're a student.

In my case:

- I wanted to take the exam **at least a week before my midterms**, so I'd have time to study for them too.
- I wanted the result early, because the scholarship deadlines I was aiming for were in November.
- I wanted a strong IELTS score on my profile before sending cold emails to professors.

I booked around the beginning of October for **Thursday, 23 October**. That gave me about 20 days. I had tried to start earlier, but I kept procrastinating and didn't get much done. Please don't copy that part. Give yourself more time than I did.

## Step 3: look at the English in your daily life

So far we only talked about your normal English level. The second factor is how much you use English in your daily life:

- Do you study in English?
- Do you listen to videos and podcasts in English?
- Do you watch movies without subtitles?
- Do you read books or articles, or use social media in English?
- Do you write your own assignments in English? Have you written a technical report before?

All of this matters, because the more you naturally use the language, the easier your prep will be. You'll mostly be getting used to applying skills you already have.

What if you do none of this? Then, as we go, we'll add it to your day little by little, one small thing at a time, until most of your day has English in it and you hardly feel it.

## Step 4: know your own habits

Some things aren't English skills, but they affect your exam:

- You zone out a lot, and you can't read two lines without re-reading them seven times.
- You type slowly on a keyboard.
- You get nervous speaking in front of people.
- You find it hard to express yourself.

These look small, but they show up during the exam. As we go through each part, I'll point out which of these skills matter there and how to work on them.

The most important one right now is **reading fast**. You'll hear a lot about skimming when we reach the Reading part, but I'm mentioning it here because you need to read questions quickly in *every* part of the exam.

## Step 5: how I trained

Before you set up your own plan, decide what your practice looks like. Do you master one skill and then move to the next? Do you work on everything together? Do you train on the full exam or on parts? Do you memorize vocabulary?

Honestly, the schedule and details are something you'll shape for yourself. What I'm sharing is my personal experience, not something to copy line by line.

**What didn't work for me:** I first took a Udemy course and went through each skill separately, in detail. I didn't get much out of it.

**What worked:** I booked my exam with the British Council and started practising straight away on their sample tests. I'd take a test, check my scores, see what I got wrong, fix it, and repeat. I prefer this approach because I'm convinced a big part of your score depends on understanding the exam's style and getting used to it. I also prefer the British Council samples over other sites, because other sites' tests tend to be either much easier or much harder than the real thing.

Throughout the practice period, we're also improving our general skills.

A side note: when you book through the British Council, you get access to **free live sessions**. Attend them. I found out late, and they were very useful.

**Where to start:** I'd start with **Listening and Reading**. These are the two sections where you should be collecting the most points, so you want to be good at them and comfortable with them. Spend about **three days on each** just to get used to the exam, understand the format, and see your level in the scores. Then increase the intensity and work on both in parallel to raise your level. Don't let it drag on, and don't get so absorbed in them that you forget Speaking and Writing.

## Quick recap

1. Understand the scoring; don't treat it as an English course.
2. Choose one main source (British Council) and support it with a few others.
3. Measure your baseline with several tests and take the average.
4. Leave at least a month if you're around B1 to B2.
5. Put English into your day gradually.
6. Work on habits too: fast reading, typing, speaking confidence.
7. Practise on real British Council samples, check your mistakes, fix, repeat.
8. Start with Listening and Reading.
