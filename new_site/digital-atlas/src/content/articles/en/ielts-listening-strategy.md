> This is my personal experience, not official advice. Test formats and rules change, so check the official British Council pages before you rely on anything here. This article is part of a series: start with [the intro](/learn/articles/ielts-start-here/) if you haven't read it.

## Where I started

When I began practising Listening, I was getting around **6 to 6.5**. My goal was **8 or higher**, and that's very possible. Honestly, it's normal. If you try your first test and get a low score, don't worry.

I did around **25 practice tests** from the British Council, maybe more, I don't remember exactly. Before the exam my practice average was about **7.5 to 8**, and in the real exam I got **8**, Alhamdulillah.

My problems at the start: spelling, zoning out in the middle of the audio, not knowing the different question types, and mixing up capital and small letters. I'll cover each one below.

## Train your ears first

Before any technique, your ears need to be used to English, ideally in different accents. How?

Reels, videos, podcasts, interviews, anything. Put English into most of your day. Don't make it exhausting: choose material close to your level, something you can listen to and understand.

## Two habits that matter in every section

**1. Read the questions fast, before the audio starts.**
You often won't have time to read all of them, but go through as many as you can. In each gap, look at the words just before and after it and try to **predict the answer** in your head. Also expect that the speaker **won't say the exact words** you see. They might use a synonym or a similar meaning, especially when the test wants to make things harder.

**2. Write while you listen.**
Not just listen. Write, in real time. This is the skill you're building across the whole Listening test. Expect to make a lot of mistakes and get confused at first. That's fine, it's how you get used to the exam. But **keep practising to the end** of each test. Never stop in the middle.

## How the test is built

There are **four sections**, each with roughly **10 questions**, and each section is **harder than the one before**. So collect as many points as you can from the early sections.

Some rules to learn well, because they apply in Listening and Reading:

- **Read the word limit carefully.** It might say a number and one word, one or two words, a number only, or one word only. It's very rare to be allowed three words.
- **Check whether numbers are allowed** in that answer.
- **Questions come in order.** Once the audio moves on to the next piece of information, it doesn't come back.
- **Capital letters:** I wrote all my answers in capital letters, which avoids confusion between capital and small letters. In my experience it wasn't a problem for marking, but check the official instructions for your format.

## Section 1: a phone call

Two people with normal accents talk about something specific: a request, a booking, an order. Think call centre. The agent asks the customer for information, and your job is to **hear the information from both of them and write it down**.

The questions are in front of you, usually a form or a table. Read them quickly before the audio so you know the topic, and decide which points you'll focus on. Not all the information is important, and not all of it will be asked.

**What the answers usually are:** colours, numbers, places, and dates. Names of cities are rare, but sometimes you'll be asked for an address. Dates can come in different forms: week number, day, or year.

So the simplest skill to build first is **hearing a number and writing it correctly**. Start with a simple video where you practise writing numbers and dates.

**How the section flows:** usually there are no two pieces of information back to back that you're asked about. There's a gap between questions. The speaker gives the answer word for word, and your job is to write as you listen.

**The correction trap:** two things can happen. Either the information is right and the other person confirms it, or the information has a mistake and the other person **corrects it**. By then you've written the first answer, or you're still writing it, and you have to fix it.

**Spelling and sounds:** you may get confused between the sounds of letters, especially **P and B**, and **M and N**. It didn't happen to me often, maybe once or twice, but you need to be able to tell them apart. Letters and numbers together can also confuse you, for example in a passport number or a postcode.

**My focus tip:** I always kept my attention on **two questions, not one**: the one I'm answering and the one coming next. If the speaker ever gives two answers back to back, I don't panic. I hold them in my head and write them quickly.

## Section 2: one speaker, multiple choice

One person explains something to a group of people: a tourist place, instructions on a bus, and so on. Here the **question setup changes**. It can be a map or a diagram. The questions are actually easy, but you need to concentrate while the person explains.

Remember: the questions are **in order**. The point you're asked about is answered, and then the speaker moves on and doesn't go back.

How I handled it:

- Before the audio starts, read the questions quickly. Not all of them, just as many as you can.
- In each question, look at the words around the gap and predict the answer. Expect synonyms.
- Usually in the first points of a question the speaker gives the information related to the answer, then a short **filler** of a sentence or two gives you time to read the next two questions and focus.
- The section may be split into parts, and between parts you get a short time to read.

## The spelling problem

The biggest problem I had in Listening was **spelling**. The words are often common or short, nothing complicated. But you get them wrong either because you don't actually know the spelling, or because you rushed and got confused typing on the keyboard.

For spelling there's no shortcut except **memorizing and practising**. And honestly, I think it's fine to practise this part a lot: listen, then type the word correctly on the computer. You'll find lots of videos and dictionaries for this, like [this playlist](https://youtube.com/playlist?list=PLrL05nHcFNrNlHfxUZ_6wkbyPmZBr73w3&si=5NVkNITj7YfXYR2a).

## Section 3: a discussion

Two people discuss a topic: a project they're preparing, or a presentation. The questions here are **multiple choice**, and usually you won't have time to read the questions and options before the audio starts.

This is where it gets harder. You need to **understand** what's being said, not just catch words, and tell the right answer from the wrong ones. Usually the two speakers agree on one answer, but they may mention the other options along the way, or one of them may agree while the other doesn't.

In the previous sections we were choosing or writing something that was said. Here we're looking for the **correct** thing, and it's not necessarily said word for word. The speaker may also finish one question and move straight into the next. All of this means you need focus and fast reading.

How I solved it:

1. Listen carefully. As soon as the speakers start the topic of the question I'm on, I **read the options again** and understand them.
2. As soon as they mention an option, I choose it.
3. If they keep talking about the same topic, I check whether the answer changes. If it changed, I change it. If not, I leave it.
4. As soon as I notice they've moved to the next question's topic, I move my focus and repeat.

While listening to the first question, take a **quick look at the next one** so you know what it's about, or remind yourself if you didn't get to read it. Then, if you hear the answer to the *next* question, you know you missed one. Don't panic: move on and keep going.

The back-and-forth where the answer changes usually happens in **two or three questions**, not in all of them.

I do this using **keywords**. I don't read the whole question. I look for one or two keywords in each question that help me understand its topic: a specific name, an exaggerating phrase, an adjective, and so on. Once the audio moves to the next question, I don't go back to anything before it.

## Section 4: a lecture

A general lecture on a topic, with one person explaining. Here again you **write while you listen**. It may be split into parts, but it has **no rule about the spacing between questions**: two questions may be back to back, with no 5-second gap, and the next two may have 20 seconds between them.

Usually you can't read all the questions before it starts. There may be a word or two where the **spelling is hard**.

The order of words may also differ from what's written. An adjective written at the end of the sentence might be said at the beginning, or a sentence written in the passive voice might be spoken in the active. This can confuse you. When you hear a word you can see at the end of the written sentence, you get a moment of tension or lost focus, thinking the answer has already passed, so you zone out.

So the key skill in this section is **knowing when the sentence has ended, and then answering**.

## How it went

In the last two sections I made lots of mistakes at first. With practice, and with getting used to writing while listening, my practice average before the exam was around **7.5 to 8**, and in the exam I got **8**, Alhamdulillah.

I had a problem with the spelling of many words, and with zoning out in the middle of the audio. But practice really does make a difference.

## Quick recap

1. Get your ears used to English in different accents, at your level.
2. Read questions fast and predict answers. Expect synonyms.
3. Write while you listen. Keep practising to the end of every test.
4. Check the word limit and whether numbers are allowed.
5. Section 1: practise numbers and dates; watch for corrections and similar sounds (P/B, M/N).
6. Always focus on the current question and the next one.
7. Sections 3 and 4: use keywords, expect paraphrase, and know when a sentence has ended.
8. Spelling: memorize and practise typing what you hear.
